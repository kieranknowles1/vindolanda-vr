\section{Introduction}

Roman occupation of Britain and Hadrian's wall plays a key part in the history
of Britain, particularly in the North-East of England. This seemed a natural
area to create a game to both educate children on history and introduce
them to the world of virtual reality (VR). The decision to use VR over a
traditional display (flatscreen) raises a number of unique challenges in
managing motion sickness, safety due to spatial awareness, and the requirement
to use motion controls instead of keyboard mappings to interact with the world.

Artistic license was taken to ease development by giving more reference material
as the ninth fort, despite not existing during Hadrian's rule, is the best
documented and preserved of the forts.

The VR ecosystem is highly fragmented, with multiple competing headsets and many
more controllers, each featuring different inputs which adds challenge to
creating input schemes compatible with different hardware, this area was deemed
impractical to address as it would require bespoke testing for each type of
controller.

The author's prior experience with playing, but not developing, VR games gave a
base level of knowledge in VR interactions such as picking up and using items,
as well as in movement types, but also limited measurement of how intuitive
these are for new users.

Virtual reality is a significantly more demanding environment than other games.
While 1080p at 60Hz is a common target for flat screens, VR games must render
two screens at 90-120Hz each, with users often upscaling for enhanced clarity.
Consequently, performance is a crucial requirement for any VR game.
(\hyperref[sec:nfr_performance]{NFR01})

Given the short deadline of the project, industry adoption was also a major
consideration based on the assumption that a more widely adopted engine would
have more resources available to reduce development time. The total number of
games tagged as VR, detected as using a specific engine by
SteamDB (\url{https://steamdb.info/tech/}), and released
after May 2023 (to include current trends) were used to determine adoption.
This methodology has its limitations: by only using SteamDB as a source,
selection bias is introduced as non-steam releases such as itch.io and the Meta
Store are excluded. Additionally, SteamDB's engine detection can produce false
negatives, for example, Godot games can be packaged as a single executable which
will not be detected as shown in Figure \ref{fig:godot_false_negative}. Despite
these limitations, this methodology was considered good enough to get a
high-level overview.

A smaller, but important, consideration was the engine's support for Git version
control. While it would have been possible to install and learn Perforce, doing
so would detract from development time, this made Unreal Engine a less practical
choice as it uses binary files for assets, which Git is poorly suited at
versioning.

Based on this analysis, it was determined that Unity best fits the needs of the
game as it is an industry standard and provides a VR template that includes all
locomotion types needed and integration with its UI system. More specifically,
Unity 6.1 was used as it was the latest stable version at time of development.

\subsubsection{Which Controllers?}

There are many distinct controllers available for VR systems with significant
variance in the inputs available, their locations, and tracking quality. Fully
supporting each would require bespoke assets for tutorials along with testing,
which is impractical due to their high cost. Therefore, only the Valve Index
controllers are fully supported with Unity and SteamVR's remapping used to
handle other controller types.

Implementations of VR in other games, especially with how they handle
locomotion was analysed as inspiration for how it could be handled here. Two
primary methods were identified that would be practical here:

% TODO: Screenshot of options menus in a couple games
\begin{itemize}
  \item Teleport - Player uses the joystick to select a location, then releases
    to teleport. Suggested to inexperienced users.
  \item Smooth (Head Forward) - Player holds the joystick to move forward relative
    to their headset. Can be more immersive but is also nauseating for inexperienced
    users. Players looking around the scene can also introduce nausea as their movement
    direction changes unexpectedly.
  \item Smooth (Controller Forward) - Similar to head forward, except movement

    need to reach behind their back, such as to retrieve an arrow from a quiver.
\end{itemize}

Given the variety of preferences among users, locomotion types should be
configurable such that allow each user may select what they are most comfortable
with, as well as to add vignette based on their needs. A sketch of the locomotion

\subsection{The Setting}

While Roman occupation of Britain spanned centuries, it was decided to set this
game during Emperor Hadrian's visit in 122 AD.
\cite[p.176]{danziger_hadrians_2006}, \cite[p.157]{moffat_wall_2009}
This setting gives the opportunity to teach about the rule of Hadrian himself
and the history leading to the construction of Hadrian's Wall, as well as other
events such as the disappearance/defeat of the ninth legion circa 120 AD.
\cite{rocha_romes_2021, hodgson_end_2021}

The DEM.Net elevation API was used to produce a height map of the fort and
surrounding area including satellite imagery. While the resolution was too low
for standalone use (~1 pixel per metre and lower still for elevation), it
provided a suitable backdrop for the scene and guided the scale of the
reproduction.

An OpenStreetMap export served as a reference for the fort's layout which could
be overlaid on the Unity editor with a script and used to guide level design.
This included the fort's exterior walls, its barracks, the granary, headquarters,
commander's quarters, and the town's strip houses and tavern. The depiction of
the wall likewise includes the wall itself and ``Castle Nick''.

\subsubsection{The Fort}

There were at least nine forts built on the site of Vindolanda, the early ones
being built out of untreated timber and demolished when their garrisons were
recalled, with the next fort being built on top of the previous's remains.
\cite{birley_vindolanda_2009} These would be garrisoned by a cohort of ten
centuries totalling eight hundred men, although not all would be present at the
fort and as much as a third would have been unfit for service, likely due to
poor hygiene, as documented in a strength report from circa AD 90.
\cite{bowman_military_1991}

\begin{figure}
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{osm-vindolandra.png}
  \caption{\label{fig:osm_map}Map of Vindolanda. Via OpenStreetMap [ODbL License]}
  \Description{A map of the Vindolanda excavations showing the layout of the
  ninth fort}

The ninth fort was constructed from stone and, although it didn't exist until
a nearly a century after Hadrian's reign and the setting of this game, it is the
best documented. As such, it and the adjacent town was chosen as the basis for
this depiction, with stone materials being replaced with the more
period-appropriate wood to compromise between ease of development and historical
accuracy. An overhead map of the fort is shown in Figure \ref{fig:osm_map}.

\subsubsection{The Wall}

In addition to the fort, Hadrian's wall through
milecastle 39, frequently referred to as ``Castle Nick'' although it is unclear
where this nickname originated. This site is also notable as being where the famous
Sycamore Gap tree once stood.

Milecastle 39 is situated approximately 1.5 kilometres north-north-west of the fort,
making it impractical to have users manually walk this distance in VR. Instead,
both Vindolanda and Castle Nick feature triggers, shown in Figure \ref{fig:teleport_trigger}

\subsection{VR Considerations}
Virtual reality introduces a unique set of challenges to game development. One
of these is motion sickness, especially for users inexperienced with VR,
\cite{chattha_motion_2020} which is often worsened by smooth locomotion, but can
be mitigated by instead using teleport locomotion, although some users may feel
disoriented after teleporting, the majority find it an easy to use movement
method. \cite{bozgeyikli_point_2016} Due to the target audience of this game
being mostly users without prior VR experience, teleport movement was deemed a
higher priority than smooth movement.

\label{sec:text_scale}
Another challenge, especially when using lower resolution headsets, is the
readability of text. Users require relatively large characters of approximately
20-25mm per metre of distance (distance independent millimetre or dmm
\cite{google_for_developers_designing_2017}) to read comfortably on the lower
resolution HTC Vive, \cite{solum_readability_2019} which is the only headset
available to the researcher and limits text to roughly 8 words per line.

Even after taking precautions to limit motion sickness, some sickness is still
to be expected. Coupled with how physically demanding VR can be compared to
other games, \hyperref[sec:fr_save]{FR07} was derived to allow for users to take
breaks as required, which they are instructed to do often and to take things
slow to reduce sickness.

Finally, there is the safety aspect to consider, namely real world boundaries and
obstacles which users may lose awareness of while in VR. Existing boundary systems
which overlay a grid when players get too close to an obstacle, remain the industry
standard and are provided automatically by platforms such as Valve's SteamVR.
\cite{valve_software_steam_nodate} and Meta's Quest \cite{meta_set_nodate}. While
these are less immersive \cite{george_invisible_2020, sanchez-vives_presence_2005}
and increase cognitive load \cite{wickens_multiple_2008} they remain effective
enough to allow safe usage of VR even in fairly small spaces. The tutorial will
be designed to inform users of this to account for players who have not used
their platform's native tutorials.

Testing was complicated by the developer's prior experience which caused
movement options and interactions to appear more intuitive than they may be and
for these systems to cause less motion sickness. This also introduced bias:
while smooth rotation may be acceptable for some users, it was not considered
here due to causing the developer significant disorientation.


\subsection{Educational Value}
Educational games are a subcategory of ``Serious Games'', which aim to use the
medium of games to teach through entertainment, and have had positive results in
prior studies, although there is limited data for their efficacy in teaching
history. \cite{backlund_educational_2013}

Prior research in VR for education has focused largely on university and adult
training with little focus on teaching children due to their more limited 3D
vision and hand-eye coordination meaning its use has to be limited to avoid
health risks. \cite{freina_literature_2015} However, it
also gives the opportunity to improve understanding about areas that
learners would physically be unable to reach such as the solar system, which
would likely be applicable to history. \cite{detlefsen_cosmic_2014}

\subsection{Tutorial}
Initial user demos were performed by users with no VR experience and no built-in
tutorial. Instead, they were instructed on how to perform tasks such as movement
and archery. This helped inform development of the tutorial required by
\hyperref[sec:fr_tutorial]{FR06}. For example, the Index controllers were less
intuitive than anticipated: users would instinctively grip the controllers
instead of holding with an open palm as intended by the controller design, which
would be registered as attempting to pick up an object. When it came time to pick
up the bow or an arrow in the demo, users struggled to do so and required
assistance.

The basic tutorial was designed with the following stages and is presented non-diegeticlly,
as it focuses on the use of VR systems such as the controllers VR rather than in-game interactions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Introduction - State the purpose of the tutorial, instruct players on how
    to repeat instructions, advise them on motion sickness, and warn about their
    play area bounds.
  \item Movement - Players move to a target marker using their selected method
    (teleport or smooth) to get them used to basic locomotion.
  \item Interaction - Players pick up an item from a table to teach them how to do
    so, a motion-captured animation is included to demonstrate how this is done.
  \item Menus - Players are instructed to open the game's menu and save their progress
    to teach them what to do when they need a break.
\end{itemize}

Afterwards, players can practice archery with a diegetic teacher. This section assumes
users are already familiar with basic interaction and thus uses terms that maintain
immersion, for example, instructing to ``pick up an arrow from the quiver on your back'' rather than
``grab your controller behind your back to take an arrow''. This teaches more complex interactions
as using a bow requires the multi-step process of taking an arrow, nocking it, drawing the string,
aiming, and releasing. As with to the basic tutorial, a motion-captured NPC is shown using a bow to
demonstrate this motion.

\subsection{Motion Capture}

Motion capture is heavily used in the film and games industries to cheaply produce
realistic animations. While this traditionally requires expensive equipment to produce, including
specialised suits or multiple cameras \cite{nogueira_motion_2011}, virtual reality can imitate
this as it provides three highly accurate tracking points: the player's head and their hands.
The Index controllers used by the developer additionally track individual finger states that
further enhance precision.

When combined with inverse kinematics, which was already implemented to position the player's body,
this results in a rudimentary yet reasonably effective motion capture system, though as expected it
sacrifices precision, making it less effective than a professional system. \cite{vox_evaluation_2021}
This is especially visible in motions such as pulling a hand to the chin to draw a bow, where inverse
kinematics positioned the elbow in front of the face rather than behind it as shown in Figure \ref{fig:hand_wrong}

\subsection{Requirement Analysis}

A requirement analysis using the MoSCoW method (Must-have, Should-have,
Could-have, and Won't-have) was performed to determine functional and
non-functional requirements for the game.

\subsubsection{Functional requirements}

\paragraph{\label{sec:fr_recreate}FR01 Recreate Vindolanda}
The game \textbf{must} include a recreation of Vindolanda during Hadrian's
rule. This was the primary goal of the proposal.

\paragraph{\label{sec:fr_wall}FR02 Recreate Hadrian's Wall}
The game \textbf{should} include a recreation of Hadrian's wall during Hadrian's
rule. This can take the form of a milecastle.

\paragraph{\label{sec:fr_beforeafter}FR03 Before and After}
The game \textbf{could} provide both past and present depictions of locations.

\paragraph{\label{sec:fr_teleport}FR04 Teleport Locomotion}
The game \textbf{must} include teleport-based locomotion. This is the most
accessible form of movement for new users.

\paragraph{\label{sec:fr_smooth}FR05 Smooth Locomotion}
The game \textbf{should} include smooth locomotion. This is a nice to have for
users more experienced in VR.

\paragraph{\label{sec:fr_tutorial}FR06 Tutorial}
The game \textbf{should} begin with a brief tutorial on how to move around
using the selected locomotion method, and advise them on the motion sickness
they may experience from smooth locomotion. This serves to introduce users to
the basics of navigating a VR world.

\paragraph{\label{sec:fr_save}FR07 Save Progress}
Given how virtual reality can cause motion sickness which require frequent
breaks, there \textbf{should} be a system to save the user's progress and return
later.

\paragraph{\label{sec:fr_lefty}FR08 Left Handed Support}
As I am left handed myself, the game \textbf{must} support both left and
right-handed interaction in order to allow comfortable use by all users and to
ease testing.

\paragraph{\label{sec:fr_mocap}FR09 Motion Capture}
The game \textbf{could} use VR input, combined with inverse kinematics, to
create basic motion-captured animations. These \textbf{could} include the ability
to mirror said animations such that I could comfortably record them for right-handed
characters in conjunction with \hyperref[sec:fr_lefty]{FR08}

\subsubsection{Non-functional requirements}

\paragraph{\label{sec:nfr_performance}NFR01 Performance}
The game \textbf{must} maintain a minimum of $45FPS$, $22.2ms$ per frame, at all
times, including during heavy load, to avoid motion sickness.

The game \textbf{should} target $90FPS$, $11.1ms$ per frame, during typical
gameplay to match the HTC Vive's native refresh rate without the need for
reprojection.

\paragraph{\label{sec:nfr_linux}NFR02 Linux Support}
The game \textbf{could} include native Linux support. This is a nice to have as
Proton is good enough for most games and getting SteamVR to work on Linux can be
difficult.

\paragraph{\label{sec:nfr_text}NFR03 Text Legibility}
Text \textbf{must} be displayed using a clear, easily readable font with high
contrast and a size of at least 25mm per metre of viewing distance to ensure
comfortable reading on the widest range of headsets, as discussed in Section
\ref{sec:text_scale}.

\paragraph{\label{sec:nfr_subtitle}NFR04 Subtitles}
All dialogue \textbf{must} provide subtitles for the player to enhance accessibility.
These \textbf{could} be toggleable for the end-user, but should be enabled by default
and opt-out for players who do not want them.

\subsection{Unity's Suitability}
Despite being the de facto standard for VR, Unity's implementation of it and
other areas left plenty to be desired and technical debt. For example, its
behaviour graph system requires distinct and incompatible events compared to the
rest of the engine which in turn required wrappers to adapt between. This
introduced extra complexity and reduced overall code quality. Additionally, the
incompatibility between its URP and HDRP pipelines required manual conversion of
assets designed for one to work with the other.

Considering these limitations, the game may have benefited from instead using
Godot, the second engine considered, as its asset library already includes
needed systems such as quests, dialogue, and more flexible behaviour trees that
could be used outside of AI.

\subsection{Performance}

Benchmarking performance for \hyperref[sec:nfr_performance]{NFR01} on desktop PCs
is inherently challenging due to the wide variety of user hardware compared to consoles
and handhelds, such as the PSVR and Meta Quest, which have a limited set of configurations.
Benchmarks were conducted on the developer's system equipped with a high-end GeForce RTX 3070
graphics card.

To approximate performance on lower-end GPUs, these benchmarks were run with a
resolution scaling factor of 400\% (four times as many pixels as normal),
significantly increasing GPU load. It should be noted that resolution scaling
is expected to have minimal CPU impact, and thus will not detect any CPU-bound
issues.

SteamVR's built-in performance graph, shown in Figure \ref{fig:vr_profile} and
the demo video (\url{https://youtu.be/CaM-xLr3iG0}) was used to display frame
times during gameplay. This data can be recorded for later review and correlated
with gameplay to identify areas with performance issues. It should be noted however,
that recording video adds a non-negligible GPU overhead that could affect results
in GPU-bound scenarios, a condition that appears to apply for most of the game.

Unexpectedly, the area with the highest frame times was Hadrian's Wall and Castle Nick
where frame times reached up to $21ms$, despite this region seemingly having
significantly less visible geometry than the fort. Further investigation suggested
two contributing factors. First, although the fort was fully culled by Unity's
occlusion system, it still incurred some overhead, though profiling proved inconclusive.
Second, the grass, used much more extensively around the wall, caused substantial
overdraw, straining VRAM bandwidth with a potentially exaggerated impact due to the 
aforementioned 400\% resolution scaling.
